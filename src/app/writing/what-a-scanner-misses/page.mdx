# What a scanner misses

A dependency scanner's job is to answer one question: is this safe to install?
The honest version of that answer is usually *probably*, and the interesting
work is in narrowing what "probably" covers.

## The shape of a false negative

Most scanners are very good at reading scripts. They parse the source, look for
the patterns that indicate exfiltration or persistence or obfuscation, and
score what they find.

The gap is that a package does not have to be source. If the payload arrives as
a compiled artefact, a scanner that only reads scripts sees a package that does
nothing interesting and says so with confidence. The verdict is clean. The
package is not.

## Why this is hard to fix properly

You cannot simply flag every dependency that ships a binary — a large fraction
of legitimate packages do, and a scanner that cries wolf gets muted, which is
strictly worse than one that misses things. False positives and false negatives
are the same problem seen from two sides.

## Past the direct dependency

The other direction is scope. A skill's dependencies are not just the packages
it declares. There are URLs it reaches for at runtime, and there are other
skills it pulls in, which have their own dependencies and their own URLs.

At that point the supply chain has stopped being a tree and become a graph, and
the question "is this safe to install" is really a question about reachability.
